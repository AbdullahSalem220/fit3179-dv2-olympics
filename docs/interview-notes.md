# Interview notes: one paragraph per chart

Marks, channels, why this idiom, and one honest limitation for each chart. Numbers match the page.

**1. Waffle chart: Paris 2024 medals by sport.** Marks: square point marks, one per medal (53). Channels: vertical position (row) for sport, which is categorical; horizontal position for order within the row; colour hue for medal type (gold, silver, bronze, which also differ in lightness so they survive greyscale). Why: a unit chart makes "53 medals" countable and shows at a glance that swimming (19) dwarfs every other sport, while still showing medal colours. Limitation: comparing two long rows exactly needs counting; a bar chart would give a more precise length comparison.

**2. Choropleth map: Paris 2024 medals per million people.** Marks: area marks (country shapes). Channel: colour lightness (a single green hue), in five classes. Why: the data is a rate per country, and a choropleth is the right idiom for a rate tied to areas; dividing by population is the standardisation the course notes require (raw counts would just repeat the medal table). The Equal Earth projection keeps relative areas true, so big countries are not visually exaggerated. Limitation: tiny nations with very high rates (Grenada 17.1, Dominica 15.1) are invisible at world scale, and large empty countries draw the eye regardless of their value.

**3. Stacked bar chart: medals per Summer Games, 1896–2024.** Marks: bars (line marks with width). Channels: horizontal position for Games (ordered); bar length on a common zero baseline for medal count; colour hue for medal type. Why: length on a common scale is the most accurate channel for comparing totals over time, and stacking shows how many of each were gold. Annotations carry the story (Melbourne 1956, Montreal 1976, AIS 1981, Sydney 2000, Paris 2024). Limitation: only the bottom segment (gold) shares a baseline, so silver and bronze are harder to compare across Games.

**4. Bump chart: medal-table rank against five rival nations, 1948–2024.** Marks: lines and point marks. Channels: vertical position for rank (reversed so 1st is at the top); horizontal position for Games; colour (green versus grey) and line width to separate Australia from the rivals; hover highlights a rival. Why: rank is ordinal, and a bump chart shows rank changes directly, here the collapse to 32nd in 1976 and top 10 at every Games since 1992. Limitation: rank hides the size of the gaps between countries; 4th by one gold medal and 4th by twenty look the same.

**5. Proportional symbol map with Europe inset: medals by host city.** Marks: circle point marks at host cities. Channels: circle area for medals won there (all Games in that city added together); colour hue for home Games (green) versus overseas (neutral grey); position for location. Why: the attribute belongs to points (cities), which suits proportional symbols. The Europe inset fixes the overlap of eight host cities that sit close together. Limitation: people judge area less accurately than length, and adding Games together hides which Games produced the medals (the tooltip shows this).

**6. Heat map: medals by sport and Games.** Marks: rectangles in a grid. Channels: horizontal position for Games, vertical position for sport (sorted by all-time total), colour lightness for medal count in five classes; numbers printed in the darker cells. Why: it shows two categorical/ordered keys at once and reveals patterns: swimming's unbroken run since 1948, and new sports appearing recently. Limitation: lightness classes lose exact values, and many empty cells use space for sports with very few medals.

**7. Treemap (Vega): all 594 Summer medals by sport.** Marks: nested rectangles. Channels: area for share of medals; colour hue for medal type in the second level; containment (spatial grouping) for sport. Why: part-to-whole with many categories; it makes "more than a third from the pool" visible immediately. Built in Vega because Vega-Lite has no treemap. Limitation: area comparisons between similar-sized blocks are imprecise, and small sports cannot be labelled (hover reveals them).

**8. Diverging bar chart: women's and men's medals per Games.** Marks: bars. Channels: length from a shared zero line (women left, men right); vertical position for Games; colour hue purple/orange (avoiding pink/blue stereotypes). Why: two counts per year that need direct comparison; the shared centre line makes "women ahead" readable in each row. Limitation: mixed (5) and open (26) medals cannot be split by gender, so they are left out.

**9. Dot plot: top 15 Olympians by gold medals.** Marks: point marks joined by a line. Channels: horizontal position on a common scale for gold medals (gold dot) and total medals (dark dot); vertical position for athlete, sorted. Why: position on a common scale is the most accurate channel, and two dots per row show both "most gold" and "most medals" without a cluttered grouped bar chart. Limitation: relay and team medals count for every team member, which favours swimmers in relays.

**10. Choropleth map: medallists per million by birth state, 2000–2024.** Marks: area marks (states). Channel: colour lightness in four wide classes. Why: a per-person rate tied to areas, standardised by population as the course notes require; an Albers equal-area projection keeps state areas true. Limitation: small populations make rates unstable: the NT's 37.2 rests on only 10 people, so one more medallist would move it to 41. Also, birth state is not where athletes trained.

**11. Bin map: birthplaces of 805 medallists, 1896–2024.** Marks: square point marks on a 1° grid. Channel: colour lightness for the count in each square, in five classes. Why: a dot map would have stacked the 126 medallists born in Sydney itself on one point, hiding the concentration; binning counts honestly without inventing dot positions. 461 of 805 (57%) were born in the five squares containing Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Perth and Adelaide. Limitation: 1° squares are not exactly equal in area (slightly smaller in the south), and 15 medallists whose town could not be matched are left off.

**12. Flow map: birth country of medallists born overseas.** Marks: curved lines (great circles) and point marks at origins. Channels: line width for number of medallists; position for origin. Why: origin–destination flows to one destination; great-circle curves on a projection centred on Australia keep every line on the map. Line ends are fanned (stopped short of the centre) so they don't pile up. Limitation: it shows birth country only, not migration routes or dates, and countries with one medallist are hard to tell apart.

**13. Small multiples: Summer and Winter medals per Games.** Marks: stacked bars in two panels. Channels: shared horizontal time position; bar length for medals (independent vertical scales); colour hue for medal type. Why: shows the very different scale of Summer and Winter success on one timeline, and Milano Cortina 2026 as the best Winter Games. Limitation: the two vertical scales differ (58 versus 6), which readers could miss; the subtitle states it.
